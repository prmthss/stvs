Old Agung had tended the lighthouse on Pelican Cove for thirty-seven years. Every evening, without fail, he would climb the iron staircase, polish the great lens, and switch on the light that guided ships safely past the treacherous rocks below. The villagers admired his dedication, though few ever visited; the lighthouse stood on a steep cliff, accessible only by a narrow, crumbling path. One October night, a violent storm rolled in from the Atlantic. Waves crashed against the cliff, and the wind howled so fiercely that the lighthouse itself seemed to tremble. Agung noticed, with growing alarm, that the main electrical generator had begun to sputter. If the light failed, the cargo freighter he had spotted on the horizon would be sailing blind into catastrophe. Agung had a backup hand-crank generator stored in the basement. It was a heavy, obsolete machine he had maintained out of habit rather than necessity. Working alone in the dark, his arthritic hands protesting every turn, he cranked the generator until the great lens blazed back to life. The freighter altered its course just in time, passing the rocks by a margin of less than a hundred metres. He found Agung sitting at his small wooden table, exhausted but composed. “How did you manage that alone?” the captain asked, clearly shaken. Agung poured two cups of tea and replied quietly, “I prepared for the worst long before the storm arrived. The backup machine was never for me. It was always for them,” and he gestured toward the sea. The captain later told the story in every port he visited. He always ended it the same way, “A man who prepares in silence serves louder than any alarm.” 1. Which excerpts from the text best describe both the main conflict and its resolution in the story?
