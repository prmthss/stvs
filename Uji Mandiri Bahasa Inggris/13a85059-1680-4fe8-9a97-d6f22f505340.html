Text 1 The Case for Four-Day School Weeks The conventional five-day school week deserves scrutiny. Research from real pilot programs in the United States offers compelling grounds for reconsideration. Economist Paul Thompson (Oregon State University, 2021) analysed Oregon districts that adopted four-day weeks. Results showed no significant decline in maths or reading scores; some rural districts even recorded modest gains, particularly where teacher vacancies had been chronic. Teacher retention is a documented benefit. A RAND Corporation report (2019) found that four-day- week districts reported higher job satisfaction and lower staff turnover, reducing costly recruitment cycles and functioning as a practical staffing solution for remote schools. Student attendance also improved. Research in the Journal of Rural Health (2017) found that flexible scheduling reduced chronic absenteeism, as families experienced less schedule-related stress. Childcare concerns are real but manageable: several districts partnered with community organizations to offer optional enrichment on the fifth day. The evidence supports context-sensitive implementation. For rural or under-resourced schools facing staffing and attendance problems, the four-day week is a reform worthy of serious consideration. Text 2 The Problem with Four-Day School Weeks Enthusiasm for four-day school weeks is understandable, but the research record reveals real limitations, especially for urban and diverse school populations. A study by Phipps et al., published in the Journal of Policy Analysis and Management (2023), examined over 900 schools across multiple US states. Students in four-day schools showed statistically lower math proficiency gains compared to peers in five-day schools, even after controlling for demographic variables. Instructional time loss matters. While proponents argue that longer daily sessions compensate, cognitive science research indicates diminishing returns after sustained learning blocks. A review by Paas et al. (Educational Psychology Review, 2020) confirmed that cognitive load accumulates during extended sessions, reducing effective learning in later hours.
