My First Science Fair: A Lesson in Unexpected Failure Last March, I participated in the Regional Science and Innovation Fair held at Universitas Diponegoro, Semarang. It was the first time our school, SMA Bagimu Negeri, had been selected to send student projects to the regional level, and I had been chosen to represent our science club with my air quality monitoring device. In the weeks leading up to the event, I assembled a small sensor array using an Arduino microcontroller, a PM2.5 particulate sensor, and a temperature-humidity module. I tested the device repeatedly in our school laboratory and was confident that it functioned accurately. My teacher, Mr. Abdi, advised me to test it outdoors in varying conditions, but I dismissed his suggestion, reasoning that the lab readings were consistent enough. On the day of the fair, I set up my display booth with carefully printed posters and a working prototype. screen froze repeatedly. Apparently, the high humidity in the exhibition hall had caused condensation inside the sensor module, corrupting the data output. I attempted to restart the device three times before the judge gently suggested we move on. I stood there, watching other participants confidently demonstrate their functioning projects, feeling both embarrassed and deeply frustrated. I did not win any award that day. On the bus journey home, Mr. Abdi sat beside me and said nothing for a long time. Then, he pointed out that my circuit design was sound and that the science behind my project was solid. I had simply failed to account for real-world environmental variables. “The best engineers,” he said, “are not those who never fail. They are those who fail in a controlled environment, so they can correct it before it matters.” That evening, I redesigned the sensor housing with a moisture-resistant casing. Three months later, the improved device won first place at the provincial level. The most important lesson I took from Semarang was not about circuits, but it was about listening. 12.
